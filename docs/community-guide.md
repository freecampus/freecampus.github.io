## Start here

You can help FreeCampus by writing a lesson, translating an explanation, testing an exercise, improving a tool, or simply telling us where you got stuck. You do not need an academic title or a long-term commitment to start.

This guide explains the different ways to participate, what to discuss before beginning, and how community contributions relate to courses in the FreeCampus App. For a small first step, visit [Contribute](contribute.md). For teaching profiles and course collaboration, explore the [Instructor Network](instructors.md).

## Two projects, different ways to contribute

**FreeCampus Community Courses** is the community effort to create and maintain open learning materials. Courses are published as public static websites with explanations, examples, exercises, a few quizzes, and learning tools. Suitable lessons may include runnable notebooks; downloadable editions are available where a course provides them. These resources support independent study without an App account or subscription.

**FreeCampus App** is a platform in development that builds richer learning experiences around selected community courses. Its direction includes structured learning paths, practice, feedback, progress tracking, and projects that help learners apply knowledge beyond the content. These are planned capabilities, not a statement that they are already available.

A community contribution improves the open course. An App collaboration may involve adapting a selected course, designing additional assessments, maintaining content, teaching, or mentoring. Selection for the App is a separate decision based on learning needs, quality, maintenance capacity, and permission to use the material. Contributing to the community does not automatically create an App role or mean every course will have an App edition.

Public community materials remain freely available independently of App subscriptions, credits, advertising, or participation. Any paid App services would support the platform experience and infrastructure. Read more [about the two projects](about.md).

## Choose a role

Jump to [authors](#authors-and-course-designers), [translators](#translators-and-localization-contributors), [reviewers](#subject-reviewers-editors-and-accessibility-contributors), [learners](#learners-and-course-testers), [developers and designers](#developers-designers-and-learning-tool-contributors), or [community helpers](#community-helpers-facilitators-and-moderators).

You can combine roles or change them as your interests develop. Start with a contribution small enough to finish and discuss larger commitments with maintainers first.

### Authors and course designers

**You can contribute:** a correction, example, lesson, exercise, quiz, project, or proposal for a new course. You can also improve the sequence of an existing course or explain a difficult prerequisite.

Before writing a substantial amount, open an issue describing the intended learners, prerequisites, learning outcomes, outline, and proposed scope. Link to related courses so maintainers can help avoid duplication. Say whether you are proposing volunteer community work or asking about a separately funded collaboration.

Build lessons around clear explanations, worked examples, practice, and useful feedback. Increase difficulty progressively. Include answers or assessment guidance where appropriate, and check that code, examples, and links work. Explain permitted AI assistance for assessed work rather than promising an AI-proof assessment.

Follow the course's source format and build process. Reuse the same source for web pages, notebooks, and downloadable editions where practical. Identify activities requiring a computer, and consider learners reading on phones or with limited connectivity. Tool-specific instructions should identify supported operating systems and explain relevant differences.

**What to expect:** discussion of scope, editorial and technical review, and revisions before publication. Acceptance depends on accuracy, suitability, licensing, and the project's ability to maintain the work. Agree on how authorship will be recorded and whether you can help with future updates; submitting a lesson does not commit you to indefinite maintenance.

### Translators and localization contributors

**You can contribute:** translations of lessons, quizzes, feedback, captions, navigation, or supporting instructions, as well as examples that make sense in another cultural context.

Before translating a full course, identify the language and locale, check for existing work, and agree on the source version and file structure. Start with one lesson and build a shared glossary for recurring terms. A translation is an adaptation: confirm the source license permits it and preserve required notices and attribution.

Translate the learning experience, including exercise instructions, answer explanations, image descriptions, and links. Keep executable code valid; translate comments or visible strings when useful, but do not change identifiers if doing so breaks examples. Ask for review of both language and subject accuracy. Machine-assisted drafts need human checking before they are ready to publish.

**What to expect:** coordination with the course maintainers, review where suitable reviewers are available, and a discussion of how source updates will be tracked. Record the source revision and known gaps. Translation availability and publication timing depend on review capacity; a volunteer translation does not create a paid contract or royalty entitlement.

### Subject reviewers, editors, and accessibility contributors

**You can contribute:** checks of technical accuracy, reasoning, prerequisites, language, assessment quality, accessibility, or learning progression. You can test whether a lesson works for its intended audience without rewriting the entire course.

Identify the lesson and version, describe the problem, and suggest a concrete improvement. For exercises, try normal cases, edge cases, and incorrect answers; check whether feedback helps someone understand the mistake. For accessibility, look at heading structure, descriptive links, image alternatives, keyboard use, contrast, and readability on small screens.

**What to expect:** discussion with authors and maintainers, with decisions recorded in the issue or pull request. Review is collaborative; explain the reasoning behind requested changes. You can review a single lesson without taking responsibility for the whole course. Do not approve work outside your expertise without identifying that limitation.

### Learners and course testers

**You can contribute:** reports of unclear explanations, broken links, confusing quizzes, missing prerequisites, or instructions that fail on your device. Share what you expected, what happened, and the lesson URL. A screenshot or minimal code example can help, but remove personal information first.

Try the material as a learner: say where you lost confidence, how long an activity took, and whether the feedback helped. Tell us your device or operating system when it matters. You do not need to know the solution to report a problem.

**What to expect:** your feedback can guide improvements, but submitting it does not guarantee an individual tutoring session, a response deadline, or immediate changes. Public community courses are designed for self-paced learning. You can ask for help without agreeing to become a contributor or joining the App.

### Developers, designers, and learning-tool contributors

**You can contribute:** course builds, notebooks, quizzes, static websites, accessibility improvements, documentation, testing, and tools that help maintainers publish from a shared source.

Read the repository instructions and check existing issues before making a substantial change. Explain the learner problem, keep the first change focused, and include relevant validation. Favor reliable loading, mobile usability, keyboard access, and low bandwidth use. Avoid adding external services or collecting learner data without first discussing the need and privacy implications with maintainers.

**What to expect:** technical review and discussion of maintenance, dependencies, and compatibility. Public course infrastructure and the App are separate projects; access to a public repository does not imply access to private App code, production systems, or learner data. App work needs its own agreed scope and access arrangements.

### Community helpers, facilitators, and moderators

**You can contribute:** welcoming newcomers, answering questions, organizing study discussions, improving onboarding, or proposing collaborative learning activities. Help people reason through a problem and credit useful sources. Respect learners' different backgrounds, languages, and available study time.

Ordinary participation does not require a formal role. Moderation authority, event ownership, and ongoing support responsibilities must be agreed with maintainers. Before announcing an activity, discuss the audience, schedule, time zone, accessibility needs, and who will run it. Discord is the preferred direction for community discussion; use only invitation links published through official FreeCampus channels.

**What to expect:** a discussion of boundaries and responsibilities for any ongoing role. Do not promise official support hours, rewards, certificates, employment referrals, or partnerships on FreeCampus's behalf. Reputation systems, helper leaderboards, and automated rewards are ideas under consideration, not benefits guaranteed for participation.

## From an idea to a published contribution

1. **Choose a project.** Browse the [subjects](subjects/index.md) and find the relevant repository in the [FreeCampus GitHub organization](https://github.com/freecampus). Read its README, contribution instructions, license, and open issues.
2. **Discuss the scope.** Small corrections can usually go straight to a pull request. For a lesson, translation, new tool, or course, open an issue first. State the audience, outcome, deliverable, available time, sources, and licensing questions.
3. **Prepare a focused contribution.** Follow the project's format. Keep a record of sources, permissions, and contributors, and explain any assistance or generated material that needs review. Never include credentials, private learner data, or material you lack permission to share.
4. **Check and submit.** Run the documented build or checks where available. Explain what changed, why it helps learners, and what you checked. If you cannot run a check, say so. A learner can report feedback in an issue without creating a pull request.
5. **Revise together.** Respond to review and resolve questions about accuracy, scope, accessibility, or rights. Maintainers decide whether to merge; proposals may need changes, be deferred, or be declined with an explanation.
6. **Plan the handoff.** Record unresolved issues and any agreed maintenance responsibility. Let maintainers know if you step away so the work can be handed over. Review and release timing depend on available capacity.

For a new course or a question that spans projects, [open an issue in the website repository](https://github.com/freecampus/freecampus.github.io/issues/new). Avoid putting sensitive information in a public issue. If you cannot use GitHub, someone may help submit your feedback with your permission; we do not currently promise a separate intake channel.

## What collaboration means

Community contributions are voluntary unless a separate agreement says otherwise. Authorship, translation, review, or helpful participation does not automatically create employment, a contractor relationship, payment, royalties, or a share of subscription revenue.

Contributors should be able to understand the scope, receive constructive review, and see the outcome of a proposal discussed openly. FreeCampus aims to provide clear project guidance and recognize contributions through the records appropriate to each course, such as commit history, contributor lists, or acknowledgments. Discuss the desired public name and attribution location when submitting work; a public instructor profile is a separate editorial decision.

We ask contributors to respect others, cite sources, raise uncertainties, and choose commitments they can sustain. Review is not a guarantee of publication or a fixed response time. If you need a deadline, regular support, or compensation, discuss it before beginning the work.

## Licensing, ownership, and attribution

### Check the exact material you want to use

There is no single assumed license for every FreeCampus resource. Read the relevant repository's LICENSE, any content-specific notices, and the terms attached to third-party images, datasets, code, or other assets. Free access by itself does not grant permission to copy, translate, adapt, or sell material.

At the time this guide was prepared, the [website](https://github.com/freecampus/freecampus.github.io/blob/main/LICENSE), [Python](https://github.com/freecampus/python/blob/main/LICENSE), and [Mathematics](https://github.com/freecampus/math/blob/main/LICENSE) repositories publish BSD 3-Clause license files. Check the current file and any exceptions for your intended use. This guide does not replace those license texts or change the license of existing work.

### Contributing original or third-party material

Before submission, confirm you have the rights needed for publication under the project's applicable license. Identify original work, copied or adapted material, sources, and any permission restrictions. If your employer, institution, coauthor, or publisher holds relevant rights, resolve permission before submitting. If the applicable license or submission terms are unclear, ask maintainers before contributing the material.

Contributing does not by itself transfer copyright to FreeCampus. Ownership and permissions follow the applicable license and any separate agreement. A nonexclusive open license lets others use work under its terms; it does not give FreeCampus exclusive ownership of every adaptation or restrict the original author's permitted uses.

### Reuse, translations, and commercial adaptations

Open licenses differ. The BSD 3-Clause text permits redistribution and use, with or without modification, subject to its conditions. Preserve the required copyright notices, conditions, and disclaimer in the form the license requires. Do not use copyright holders' or contributors' names to imply endorsement without permission. Check separately licensed assets and other permissions as well.

An App course may build on community material only when its license permits the intended reuse and commercial adaptation, or additional permission has been obtained. Credit contributors and link to the original sources. Keep a record of the source version, modifications, license, and required notices so attribution survives publication in each format.

Charging for platform services does not cancel an open license or create an automatic obligation to pay royalties. Likewise, an open source course does not automatically make all App software, additional materials, or services available under the same license. Check the terms for each item. Public community materials continue to be available independently of the App.

## Paid work and courses in the App

If you want to create, translate, maintain, teach, or mentor for an App course, raise that interest explicitly before starting. Volunteer community participation and contracted work should have separate, clear expectations. There is no published standard rate or automatic revenue share in this guide.

A paid collaboration needs a separate agreement covering deliverables, review and acceptance, schedule, compensation and payment arrangements, attribution, licenses and rights, maintenance, and how changes or cancellation will be handled. Teaching and mentoring also need agreed hours, time zones, learner capacity, and response expectations. Do not treat an exploratory discussion as a confirmed contract or promise of App publication.

You can contribute to the open community without seeking an App role. You can also discuss a potential paid collaboration without committing to unpaid preparatory work. Any arrangement must preserve the existing rights and license obligations of the community materials it uses.

## A useful first message

Introduce yourself with the role you are interested in, the course or topic, and a small contribution you would like to make. Include the intended learners, language or locale where relevant, source links, licensing questions, and any availability constraints. Say whether you are volunteering or asking about paid work. No résumé or sensitive personal details are needed for a small community contribution.
